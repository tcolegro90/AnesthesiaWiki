Bronchospasm

1. Recognition (Clinical Events)

Wheezing
Increased mucus secretion
High inspiratory pressures
Blunted expiratory CO2 waveform
Hypoxemia
Air trapping / lung hyperinflation
Differential: Pneumothorax, ETT obstruction or kinking, endobronchial intubation, anaphylaxis, pulmonary edema, and aspiration.

2. Treatment (Action Plan)

1
Deepen anesthesia with a volatile agent, ketamine, propofol, lidocaine, or a combination that rapidly increases anesthetic depth.
2
Administer 100% oxygen.
3
Give a short-acting beta-agonist. Typical rescue dosing: albuterol 4 to 8 puffs by MDI through the circuit or 2.5 to 5 mg nebulized.

    In the meantime, if time allows, add ipratropium 0.25 to 0.5 mg nebulized every 20 minutes for up to 3 doses in moderate to severe cases.
4
If severe, give epinephrine 10 mcg/kg IV or subcutaneously.
5
Give hydrocortisone 2 to 4 mg/kg IV.
6
If still refractory, consider magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over 20 minutes. Consider aminophylline selectively if prolonged postoperative ventilation is expected.
Important: Before treating this as isolated bronchospasm, rule out mechanical obstruction, endobronchial intubation, pneumothorax, and other causes of wheezing.

Ventilation Strategy

Avoid lung hyperinflation and barotrauma.
  • Reduce minute ventilation if needed.
  • Limit inspiratory time and prolong expiratory time.
  • Accept moderate permissive hypercapnia when appropriate.
  • Watch for rising intrathoracic pressure, reduced venous return, and worsening hemodynamics.
Clinical Note: Repeated bronchodilator doses may eventually plateau in benefit. Escalate thoughtfully and trend airway pressures, capnography, oxygenation, and hemodynamics closely.
Citation: Nagelhout and Elisha, Nurse Anesthesia, 6th edition.
