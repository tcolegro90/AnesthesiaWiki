# Hydralazine

## Quick Facts
- Generic: Hydralazine hydrochloride
- Class: Direct-acting vasodilator (arterial)
- Controlled substance: No
- Last updated: 2026-04-04

## Indication-Specific Guidance

### 1) Acute Hypertension Management (Intraoperative / Perioperative)
#### Typical Dosing
- 2.5 to 20 mg IV in incremental doses
#### Practical Considerations
- Onset is slow (5–15 min IV) and duration is long and unpredictable (3–7 hours)
- NOT first-line for acute intraoperative hypertension control — labetalol or nicardipine are preferred for titratable response
- Useful when a sustained reduction in BP is desired (e.g., eclampsia, post-op hypertension)

## Onset and Duration
- Onset IV: 5 to 15 minutes
- Duration: 3 to 7 hours (long and unpredictable)

## Mechanism of Action
- Pure **arterial vasodilator** — relaxes arteriolar smooth muscle via direct action, decreasing systemic vascular resistance

## Contraindications / Avoid
- Coronary artery disease where reflex tachycardia would be harmful
- Dissecting aortic aneurysm (relative)

## Key Warnings and Cautions
- **Reflex tachycardia**: Arterial dilation drops BP → baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic stimulation → tachycardia. May worsen myocardial O₂ demand in CAD patients.
- Long and **unpredictable** duration makes it difficult to titrate — dose stacking risk
- **Lupus-like syndrome**: Prolonged oral use; rare with short-term IV use
- Patients with renal or hepatic disease may have significantly prolonged elimination

## Common Adverse Effects
- Reflex tachycardia
- Flushing
- Headache
- Hypotension (overshoot)

## Monitoring
- Blood pressure and heart rate frequently after each dose

## Notes for Local Customization
- Clinical rule: Hydralazine = pure arterial dilator; Nitroglycerin = pure venous dilator.
