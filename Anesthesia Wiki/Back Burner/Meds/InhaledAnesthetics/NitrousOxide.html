Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)

Quick Facts

  • Generic: Nitrous oxide
  • Common name: Laughing gas
  • Class: Inorganic inhaled anesthetic / analgesic
  • Controlled substance: No (scheduled in some jurisdictions)
  • Last updated: 2026-04-04

Indication-Specific Guidance

1) Anesthetic Adjunct (MAC Reduction)

Typical Dosing

  • Typically 50% to 70% N₂O in O₂

Practical Considerations

  • Reduces the required concentration of volatile agent or IV agents (MAC-sparing effect)
  • Does not provide adequate anesthesia alone (MAC ~104% — cannot reach 1 MAC in room air)

2) Analgesia / Procedural Sedation

Typical Dosing

  • 30% to 50% in O₂ for conscious sedation / analgesia

Practical Considerations

  • Rapid onset and offset (Blood/gas = 0.46 — similar to desflurane)
  • Commonly used in dentistry and minor procedures

Onset and Duration

  • Blood/gas coefficient: 0.46 (very low — rapid onset and offset)
  • Full MAC equivalent (104%) cannot be achieved safely in clinical practice

Mechanism of Action

  • NMDA receptor antagonism (primary analgesic mechanism)
  • Also has GABA-A enhancement at high concentrations
  • Provides analgesia, light sedation, and euphoria

Contraindications / Avoid

  • Bowel obstruction / bowel surgery: N₂O diffuses into gas-filled cavities ~34× faster than nitrogen can leave, causing cavity expansion — dangerous in distended bowel
  • Middle / inner ear surgery: expansion of air in middle ear space can cause barotrauma
  • Pneumothorax: gas expansion is dangerous
  • Vitreoretinal surgery: if intraocular gas tamponade (SF₆, C₃F₈) is present, N₂O can catastrophically expand the bubble — typically avoided for several weeks post vitrectomy
  • Air embolism risk: N₂O will rapidly enlarge any intravascular air
  • Vitamin B12 / folate deficiency: Inactivates methionine synthase — avoid in patients with B12 deficiency or megaloblastic anemia
  • Pregnancy (first trimester): theoretical teratogenicity concern; generally avoided

Key Warnings and Cautions

  • Diffusion hypoxia on emergence: when N₂O is discontinued, it rapidly enters the alveoli, diluting O₂ — administer 100% O₂ for at least 5 minutes after case
  • PONV: N₂O independently increases PONV risk
  • Occupational exposure concerns for operating room staff

Common Adverse Effects

  • PONV (increased incidence)
  • Diffusion hypoxia on emergence if not properly managed
  • Bone marrow suppression with prolonged exposure (due to B12 inactivation)

Monitoring

  • SpO₂ — ensure adequate oxygenation
  • End-tidal N₂O and CO₂ monitoring

Important Interactions

  • MAC-sparing with all volatile agents and IV anesthetics
  • 70% N₂O reduces volatile MAC requirement by approximately 0.5 MAC

Special Populations

  • Bowel, ear, eye, and thoracic surgery: contraindicated
  • PONV high-risk patients: consider avoiding N₂O

Notes for Local Customization

  • Institutional availability and environmental considerations (high global warming potential) are increasingly limiting N₂O use.
