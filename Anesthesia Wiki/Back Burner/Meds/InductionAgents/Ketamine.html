Ketamine

Quick Facts

  • Generic: Ketamine
  • Brand: Ketalar
  • Class: Dissociative anesthetic / NMDA antagonist
  • Controlled substance: Yes (Schedule III in the US)
  • Last updated: 2026-04-04

Indication-Specific Guidance

1) Induction of General Anesthesia

Typical Dosing

  • 1 to 2 mg/kg IV

Practical Considerations

  • Increases BP by ~28% and HR by ~33% on induction — advantageous in hemodynamically unstable patients
  • Drug of choice for hypovolemic / trauma induction alongside etomidate
  • Pretreat with glycopyrrolate to reduce salivary secretions

2) Procedural Sedation / Dissociative Dose

Typical Dosing

  • 0.5 to 1 mg/kg IV; 2 to 4 mg/kg IM

Practical Considerations

  • Maintains airway reflexes at lower doses
  • Emergence delirium more common without benzodiazepine co-administration

3) Sub-Dissociative Analgesia

Typical Dosing

  • 0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg IV

Practical Considerations

  • Opioid-sparing adjunct for painful procedures or multimodal analgesia
  • Much lower incidence of emergence side effects at these doses

4) Bronchospasm / Reactive Airway Induction

Typical Dosing

  • 1 to 2 mg/kg IV

Practical Considerations

  • Causes bronchodilation via sympathomimetic catecholamine release — excellent choice for asthmatic or RAD patients

Onset and Duration

  • Onset IV: 2 to 4 minutes
  • Peak effect: about 1 minute IV
  • Duration: 15 to 30 minutes IV

Mechanism of Action

  • NMDA receptor antagonist (blocks glutamate transmission)
  • Also provides analgesia, amnesia, and sympathomimetic cardiovascular effects

Contraindications / Avoid

  • Increased intracranial pressure (ICP): Ketamine increases ICP and cerebral metabolic rate (CMRO2); avoid in neuro cases / TBI unless ventilated and controlled
  • Uncontrolled hypertension (relative)
  • Known hypersensitivity

Key Warnings and Cautions

  • Vivid hallucinations and emergence delirium — co-administer benzodiazepine (e.g., midazolam) to reduce incidence
  • Salivation: activates salivary glands — pretreat with glycopyrrolate
  • Raises ICP and IOP — avoid in open globe injury and uncontrolled intracranial hypertension
  • Cardiovascular stimulating effects may be harmful in ischemic heart disease (increased O₂ demand)

Common Adverse Effects

  • Emergence delirium / hallucinations
  • Increased secretions
  • Tachycardia, hypertension
  • Nystagmus

Monitoring

  • Blood pressure, heart rate (expect increase)
  • Airway patency at procedural sedation doses

Important Interactions

  • Benzodiazepines: reduce emergence delirium
  • Glycopyrrolate: reduces secretions
  • Volatile anesthetics: additive CNS depression

Preparation and Administration Notes

  • Available in 10 mg/mL, 50 mg/mL, 100 mg/mL concentrations
  • Dilute high-concentration vials for IV use

Reversal / Overdose Strategy

  • No direct reversal agent
  • Support airway; benzodiazepines for emergence reactions

Special Populations

  • Pediatrics: widely used for procedural sedation (IM route common)
  • Trauma/hemodynamically unstable: drug of choice

Notes for Local Customization

  • Institutional protocols vary on routine benzodiazepine co-administration.
