Fentanyl

Quick Facts

  • Generic: Fentanyl
  • Brand examples: Sublimaze and others
  • Class: Opioid analgesic
  • Controlled substance: Yes (Schedule II in the US)
  • Last updated: 2026-04-04

Indication-Specific Guidance

1) Blunting Response to Laryngoscopy/Intubation

Typical Dosing

  • Often around 1 to 2 mcg/kg IV before stimulus (practice dependent)

Practical Considerations

  • Give with enough lead time for peak effect
  • Rapid/high dose can increase chest wall rigidity risk

2) Intraoperative Analgesic Adjunct

Typical Dosing

  • Incremental boluses (for example 25 to 100 mcg IV), titrated to surgical stimulus and physiology

Practical Considerations

  • Balance analgesia with respiratory and hemodynamic effects
  • Co-administered sedatives/anesthetics lower dose requirements

3) High-Analgesic Techniques / Major Stimulus Cases

Typical Dosing

  • Larger cumulative dosing may be used per provider/institution plan

Practical Considerations

  • Requires close ventilation and hemodynamic monitoring
  • Be prepared for delayed emergence and postoperative respiratory monitoring needs

4) Postoperative Analgesia Context

Typical Dosing

  • Small IV titrated doses in monitored settings (institution specific)

Practical Considerations

  • Watch for recurrent sedation/respiratory depression as other agents wear off
  • Consider multimodal strategies to reduce opioid burden

Onset and Duration

  • Onset IV: 1 to 2 minutes
  • Peak effect: about 3 to 5 minutes
  • Duration (single IV bolus): about 30 to 60 minutes

Mechanism of Action

  • Potent mu-opioid receptor agonist

Contraindications / Avoid

  • Significant respiratory depression without airway/ventilatory support
  • Known hypersensitivity (rare)

Key Warnings and Cautions

  • Respiratory depression and apnea
  • Chest wall rigidity at high/rapid doses (especially with rapid IV push)
  • Bradycardia and hypotension (less histamine release than morphine)
  • Increased sensitivity in elderly/frail patients

Common Adverse Effects

  • Respiratory depression
  • Sedation
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Bradycardia
  • Pruritus (less common with IV bolus than neuraxial exposure)

Monitoring

  • Continuous pulse oximetry and cardiorespiratory monitoring
  • Ventilation assessment (capnography where available)
  • Frequent reassessment of pain and sedation level

Important Interactions

  • Additive sedation/respiratory depression with benzodiazepines, propofol, other opioids
  • Caution with other CNS depressants

Preparation and Administration Notes

  • Standard concentration often 50 mcg/mL (verify local supply)
  • Prefer slow IV titration to effect when feasible
  • Avoid rapid large pushes unless clinically required and controlled

Reversal / Overdose Strategy

  • Naloxone titration to restore ventilation while minimizing withdrawal/pain flare
  • Airway support, oxygen, ventilation as needed
  • Ongoing observation due to potential renarcotization

Special Populations

  • Elderly: lower starting doses and slower titration
  • Obesity/OSA: increased respiratory risk, careful monitoring
  • Renal/hepatic impairment: use clinical titration and close monitoring

Documentation Checklist

  • Indication and target effect
  • Dose, route, time, and response
  • Sedation/respiratory status after administration
  • Rescue actions (if any) and follow-up reassessment
